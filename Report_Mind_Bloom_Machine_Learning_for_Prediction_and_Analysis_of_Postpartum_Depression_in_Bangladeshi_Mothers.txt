\begin{abstract}
Postpartum depression (PPD) affects an estimated 10--20\% of mothers worldwide, with even higher prevalence consistently reported in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) where structured postnatal mental health screening remains limited or completely absent \cite{Saqib2021}. This paper presents MIND-BLOOM, an interpretable machine learning framework for early PPD prediction that is designed to be applicable across diverse health systems, and is demonstrated through a detailed case study using an open Bangladeshi survey dataset (n=800) containing EPDS/PHQ scores and extensive socio-demographic, familial, obstetric, and neonatal features \cite{Raisa2025}. Building on recent explainable PPD risk models developed in Chinese tertiary hospitals \cite{Zhang2025,Qi2025,Huang2025}, MIND-BLOOM employs calibrated logistic regression, support vector machines (SVMs), and tree-based ensembles (random forest, XGBoost) together with SHAP explanations, while explicitly addressing LMIC challenges through culturally adapted feature sets, careful handling of class imbalance, and subgroup fairness analyses. The evaluation framework combines stratified training, validation, and testing splits, nested cross-validation, and decision curve analysis to optimize clinically meaningful risk thresholds that remain robust across different health system resource levels. By integrating globally relevant risk factors with locally grounded data, interpretable algorithms, and deployment considerations for low-resource maternal health services, MIND-BLOOM aims to support scalable PPD screening, risk stratification, and referral worldwide, with particular emphasis on LMIC contexts.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Postpartum depression (PPD) is a common and debilitating mental health condition that undermines maternal functioning, infant bonding, and long-term family well-being. Global estimates suggest that 10--20\% of mothers experience clinically significant depressive symptoms in the first year after childbirth, with prevalence rising to 47.8--60.6\% in several LMIC facility-based studies where social stressors and limited mental health services co-occur \cite{Islam2025, AlMamun2024, Saqib2021, Nasralla2024}. Despite this substantial burden, routine PPD screening and standardized risk assessment are implemented inconsistently across health systems, and are particularly scarce in resource-limited settings where mothers' mental health is often under-prioritized \cite{Gausia2009, WHO2025}.

MIND-BLOOM addresses the universal challenge of early PPD identification and risk stratification using interpretable machine learning, while explicitly recognizing the constraints and needs of LMIC health systems. The framework is designed to predict individual-level PPD risk using routinely obtainable socio-demographic, familial, obstetric, and neonatal features that can be collected in both primary care and hospital settings, making it adaptable to diverse global contexts. A detailed case study in Bangladesh---a lower-middle-income country with documented high PPD prevalence and limited specialized mental health resources---is used to instantiate and evaluate the framework \cite{Raisa2025, Islam2025, NIMH2019}.

The proposed approach integrates global evidence from recent hospital-based ML models for PPD prediction \cite{Zhang2025,Qi2025,Huang2025} with an openly available Bangladeshi dataset of 800 postpartum women that includes EPDS, PHQ-2, PHQ-9, and a rich set of psychosocial and clinical risk factors \cite{Raisa2025}. Within MIND-BLOOM, supervised algorithms---regularized logistic regression, SVM, random forest, and XGBoost---are trained under stratified evaluation protocols that preserve outcome prevalence and key subgroups. SHAP is employed to generate both global feature importance profiles and patient-level explanation plots that can be interpreted by clinicians, nurses, and community health workers \cite{Zhang2025}. Models are benchmarked across several criteria, including discrimination (AUC, sensitivity, specificity, F1-score), calibration (Brier score and calibration curves), and clinical utility via decision curve analysis to determine context-appropriate risk thresholds \cite{Huang2025}.

Prior global work on PPD prediction includes a series of retrospective studies from Chinese tertiary hospitals that achieved AUCs between 0.80 and 0.96 using EMR data enriched with laboratory and imaging markers, combined with techniques such as LASSO, XGBoost, and SHAP \cite{Zhang2025,Qi2025,Huang2025}. These models demonstrate the potential of explainable ML for perinatal mental health but depend heavily on diagnostic infrastructure that is often unavailable in LMICs. In Bangladesh, epidemiological studies have documented a high prevalence of PPD and associated risk factors, such as income dissatisfaction, pregnancy complications, delivery-related expenditures, low gravidity, and strained family relationships, using a traditional logistic regression approach. However, these findings have not been translated into deployable ML-based risk tools \cite{Islam2025,AlMamun2024,Azad2019}. An M.Sc.\ thesis from United International University explores ML-based prediction of suicidal ideation among women with PPD, reporting AUC values between 0.86 and 0.98 for decision trees and random forests. Still, the underlying dataset is non-public, and the evaluation design is not aligned with clinical deployment requirements \cite{Zohora2025}.

Systematic reviews of ML in maternal mental health highlight the frequent use of SVMs, random forests, and gradient-boosted trees across data types such as EMRs, psychometric scales, and mobile sensing, while also emphasizing that LMIC populations remain markedly under-represented and that many studies neglect calibration, fairness assessment, and implementation considerations \cite{Saqib2021}. Taken together, the literature points to several key gaps: (1) a lack of open, multi-site LMIC datasets that combine standardized PPD measures with rich psychosocial and clinical features \cite{Raisa2025}; (2) insufficient attention to longitudinal risk trajectories and subgroup fairness across socio-economic and geographic strata; and (3) minimal integration of explainable ML models into the day-to-day workflows of overburdened primary care and maternal health services \cite{Gausia2009,Dutta2022}.

\subsection*{Research Novelties}

The novelties of MIND-BLOOM are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A globally applicable PPD prediction framework that can be instantiated on different datasets and health systems, demonstrated here with Bangladeshi EPDS/PHQ data as a representative LMIC case study \cite{Raisa2025,Islam2025}.
    \item Combined use of calibrated logistic regression, SVM, and tree-based ensembles with SHAP explanations, explicitly tuned to provide clinician- and community health worker-friendly insights rather than opaque black-box predictions \cite{Zhang2025,Huang2025}.
    \item A comprehensive evaluation protocol incorporating stratified train--validation--test splits, nested cross-validation, calibration metrics, and decision curve analysis so that decision thresholds can be tailored to different resource settings and clinical objectives \cite{Huang2025}.
    \item Systematic subgroup and fairness analyses across socio-economic, geographic, and obstetric subgroups, with a particular focus on the types of inequities commonly observed in LMIC health systems \cite{Islam2025,Saqib2021}.
    \item A deployment-oriented design that explicitly considers stigma, workforce shortages, and referral capacity, linking model outputs to feasible care pathways in community and facility-based maternal health services \cite{Dutta2022,Gausia2009}.
\end{itemize}

\section{Literature Review}

\subsection{Global Burden of Postpartum Depression}

PPD prevalence varies widely across regions, typically ranging from 10--20\% in high-income settings to 47.8--60.6\% in several LMIC studies that include both community and facility-based samples \cite{Saqib2021,Islam2025,AlMamun2024}. Urban Bangladesh studies, for example, report 60.6\% prevalence using an EPDS cut-off of 10 among mothers up to two years postpartum, and link this high burden to income dissatisfaction, pregnancy complications, frequent antenatal care (ANC) visits, and high childbirth-related expenditures \cite{AlMamun2024}. A multi-site study of Bangladeshi parturients identified a 47.8\% prevalence of PPD, with 29.1\% meeting criteria for major PPD, and highlighted socio-demographic and obstetric risk factors such as unplanned pregnancy and relationship stressors \cite{Islam2025}. Rural investigations indicate that many women with significant distress do not seek formal mental healthcare because of stigma, lack of recognition of mental health problems, and limited availability of appropriate services \cite{Dutta2022,Gausia2009}.

\subsection{International Machine Learning for PPD Prediction}

Several influential studies from Chinese tertiary hospitals have developed interpretable ML models for PPD prediction. Zhang et al.\ used a cohort of 2,055 women and applied LASSO feature selection followed by XGBoost with SHAP explanations, reporting an AUC of approximately 0.85 and identifying antepartum depression, fetal weight, and thyroid and iron markers as key predictors \cite{Zhang2025}. Qi et al.\ recruited 1,138 perinatal women and compared prenatal and postpartum models across multiple algorithms, finding that logistic regression achieved AUCs of 0.80--0.86. That postpartum caregiver-related variables significantly improved predictive performance \cite{Qi2025}. Huang et al.\ analyzed 1,065 women at six-week postpartum visits, combining LASSO and Boruta feature selection with a panel of eight algorithms, and reported XGBoost performance of AUC 0.955. However, without external validation in other settings \cite{Huang2025}. Systematic reviews of maternal mental health ML methods show that SVM and random forest are widely used across modalities, but also emphasize that LMIC populations are rarely represented and that many models omit calibration metrics and real-world deployment analyses \cite{Saqib2021}.

\subsection{LMIC Data Resources and Local Efforts}

In the LMIC context, the Mendeley Bangladesh PPD dataset (n=800) stands out as a rare open resource that includes EPDS, PHQ-2, PHQ-9, and detailed socio-demographic, familial, health, and neonatal variables, with 44\% of participants flagged as high risk for PPD based on EPDS thresholds \cite{Raisa2025}. This dataset was developed using expert-validated questionnaires and standardized screening tools, making it particularly suitable for reproducible ML research grounded in local realities. Complementary cross-sectional studies in urban facilities and slum settings contribute additional, though non-public, datasets and collectively reinforce the importance of economic stress, pregnancy complications, adverse obstetric history, and interpersonal relations as determinants of PPD \cite{AlMamun2024,Azad2019,Islam2025}. At the ML application level, the UIU thesis evaluated decision trees, logistic regression, random forest, and SVM for predicting PPD-related suicidal ideation, achieving AUCs between 0.86 and 0.98 but relying on a high-risk clinical sample and data that are not currently shared \cite{Zohora2025}.

\subsection{Explainability and Deployment Challenges}

Explainability methods, particularly SHAP, have become central in recent PPD prediction studies because they allow clinicians to visualize how risk factors such as antepartum depression, family support, thyroid function, and socio-economic status contribute to individual predictions \cite{Zhang2025,Huang2025}. However, most of this work has been conducted in tertiary hospitals with specialist staff, and there is limited evidence about how effectively front-line providers and community health workers can use these explanation tools in LMIC settings. Global evaluations of PPD ML models frequently prioritize internal cross-validation accuracy while neglecting calibration, decision curve analysis, and subgroup performance across socio-economic and geographic strata, which are crucial for safe deployment in heterogeneous populations \cite{Saqib2021}. Furthermore, LMIC deployment must contend with pervasive stigma, limited mental health literacy, and weak referral pathways, raising ethical concerns about introducing predictive tools without adequate systems for counseling, follow-up, and treatment \cite{Dutta2022,Gausia2009}.

The proposed methodology for the MIND-BLOOM framework is visualized in Fig. \ref{fig:mindbloom_framework}. This section outlines the workflow from data acquisition to model deployment, highlighting the integration of machine learning and deep learning architectures.

The framework is structured into five core phases:

\subsection{Data Acquisition and Preprocessing}
% === [Requirement: b] Dataset Details ===
The study utilizes the Mendeley PPD dataset ($n=800$) collected from Bangladesh, containing rich socio-demographic, obstetric, and psychometric attributes including EPDS and PHQ-9 scores \cite{Raisa2025}. 
% ========================================

Raw data undergoes a rigorous preprocessing pipeline. Missing values are imputed, and One-Hot Encoding is applied to transform qualitative survey responses into numerical vectors. To ensure data quality, outliers are detected and handled, followed by correlation analysis to identify and drop redundant features that could introduce multicollinearity. Table \ref{tab:dataset_features} summarizes the key features used.

\subsection{Stratified Splitting and Balancing}
A critical step in our methodology is the prevention of data leakage. The dataset is first divided into training and testing sets. Subsequently, Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE) is applied \textit{only} to the training data. This ensures that the synthetic samples used to balance the classes do not bleed into the test set, preserving the integrity of the evaluation.

\subsection{Dual-Branch Model Development}
% === [Requirement: c] Model Techniques ===
The framework employs two parallel modeling branches to capture diverse data patterns:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Machine Learning Branch:} Utilizes traditional algorithms including Random Forest, XGBoost, Logistic Regression, K-nearest Neighbors (KNN), Support Vector Machines (SVMs), and Decision Trees.
    \item \textbf{Deep Learning Branch:} Incorporates advanced architectures using PyTorch, specifically leveraging BERT models to capture complex, non-linear dependencies in the data.
\end{itemize}
% =========================================

% === [Requirement: e] Tech Stack ===
The Machine Learning branch is implemented using the \textbf{Scikit-learn} library, while data manipulation relies on \textbf{Pandas} and \textbf{NumPy}. The Deep Learning components are built upon the \textbf{PyTorch} framework.
% ===================================

\subsection{Optimization and Ensemble Learning}
Hyperparameter Optimization is conducted to fine-tune the models. To maximize predictive performance, a robust Ensemble strategy is implemented, combining Random Forest, XGBoost, SVMs, and Logistic Regression. The final decision is derived using both Soft Voting (averaging predicted probabilities) and Hard Voting (majority class selection) to produce a stratified risk classification (Low, Medium, High).

\subsection{Evaluation and Deployment}
The final classification results are evaluated using a comprehensive suite of metrics. In addition to standard Accuracy, Precision, Recall (Sensitivity), and F1-Score, we incorporate \textbf{Specificity}, \textbf{AUC-ROC}, and \textbf{Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC)} to ensure robust performance assessment despite class imbalance. A Confusion Matrix is also generated to visualize error distribution.

% === [Requirement: d] User Interface & Feasibility ===
To ensure clinical utility, the system includes Explainable AI (XAI) for interpretability. The final deployment phase integrates the model into a Survey Web/App coupled with a Chatbot Interface (Fig. \ref{fig:ui_mockup}). This hybrid deployment strategy ensures feasibility in low-resource LMIC settings by minimizing the need for immediate clinical staffing while leveraging the high mobile penetration rates in the region.
